Data leakage is the most consequential mistake in applied machine learning, and spotting it unprompted is one of the strongest signals you can give in a data science interview. It produces excellent validation scores and a model that fails immediately in production.

**The definition.** Leakage is any situation where information that would not be available at prediction time influences training or evaluation. Two broad families:

**1. Target leakage** — a *feature* encodes the outcome.
**2. Train/test contamination** — the *evaluation* saw data it should not have.

**Target leakage examples.**
- Predicting churn with a `days_since_account_closed` field. It only exists after churning.
- Predicting fraud with a `chargeback_amount` column populated during investigation.
- Predicting a diagnosis from a `treatment_prescribed` field.
- Predicting conversion with a `total_lifetime_spend` computed after the conversion window.

The diagnostic question for every feature: **would this value exist, with this value, at the exact moment the prediction must be made?** If it is populated by a downstream process, it leaks.

A practical tell: a single feature with implausibly high importance, or a validation score that is suspiciously good. Investigate rather than celebrate.

**Train/test contamination: the preprocessing trap.** This is the one people commit most often.

```python
# WRONG - the scaler saw the test set
scaler.fit(X_all)
X_all_scaled = scaler.transform(X_all)
X_train, X_test = split(X_all_scaled)

# RIGHT - fit on train only
X_train, X_test = split(X_all)
scaler.fit(X_train)
X_train_scaled = scaler.transform(X_train)
X_test_scaled = scaler.transform(X_test)     # same fitted parameters
```

Any **fitted** transformer is subject to this rule, not just scalers:
- Scalers (the mean and std are learned)
- Imputers (the fill value is learned)
- Encoders (the category set is learned)
- Feature selection (which features survive is learned)
- Dimensionality reduction such as PCA
- Resampling such as SMOTE — oversample the training fold only

**The practical answer is a Pipeline.** Wrapping preprocessing and the estimator in a `sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline` and passing that to `cross_val_score` refits every step on each training fold automatically. This is the main reason Pipelines exist, and saying so is the right answer to "how do you prevent this?"

**Time-series leakage.** A random split lets the model train on the future and predict the past — a situation the deployed model never faces.

- Use a **forward-chaining** split: train on [0, t], validate on (t, t+h], roll forward.
- Watch **feature construction** too. A centered rolling window (`center=True`) includes future values. A `rolling(7).mean()` used as a feature must be trailing.
- Beware of a **gap** requirement: if predictions are made a week ahead, leave a week between train and validation, or you leak information the model would not yet have.

**Group leakage.** If the same entity appears in both train and validation — several visits from one patient, several sessions from one user, several photos of one object — the model can memorize the entity rather than learn the pattern. Use `GroupKFold` or `GroupShuffleSplit` keyed on the entity id.

**Duplicate leakage.** Exact or near-duplicate rows split across train and test inflate scores. Deduplicate *before* splitting.

**Cross-validation, correctly.**

```
k-fold:             each sample is validated once, trained on k-1 times
StratifiedKFold:    preserves class proportions per fold - essential when imbalanced
GroupKFold:         keeps an entity entirely within one fold
TimeSeriesSplit:    forward-chaining, never trains on the future
```

With 1% positives and plain 5-fold, a fold can contain almost no positives, making its metrics meaningless and the overall estimate very high-variance. Stratify.

**Why cross-validate at all rather than use one split?** A single split gives one noisy estimate that depends heavily on which rows landed where. k-fold uses every observation for both training and validation (never simultaneously), giving a lower-variance estimate plus a spread across folds — and that spread is itself informative. Large fold-to-fold variance means your estimate is unstable and you should not trust a small difference between two models.

**The three-way split.** Train / validation / test exist for distinct purposes:
- **Train** fits parameters.
- **Validation** selects hyperparameters and models.
- **Test** is touched **once**, at the end, to estimate generalization.

Tuning against the test set turns it into a validation set, and your reported number becomes optimistic. If you have tuned against it repeatedly, you no longer have an unbiased estimate — say so rather than pretending otherwise.

**Overfitting vs underfitting, diagnosed from two numbers.**

| | Train error | Validation error |
|---|---|---|
| Underfitting (high bias) | high | high, close to train |
| Good fit | low | low, small gap |
| Overfitting (high variance) | very low | much higher — large gap |

The remedies are opposite, which is why the diagnosis matters. Overfitting wants regularization, more data, a simpler model, early stopping, or bagging. Underfitting wants more capacity, better features, or less regularization — and notably, **more data does not fix underfitting**, because more rows reduce variance rather than bias.

**A checklist to run on any modelling problem.**
1. For each feature: would it exist at prediction time?
2. Is every transformer fit on training data only?
3. Is preprocessing inside the cross-validation loop (a Pipeline)?
4. Is the split chronological if the data is temporal?
5. Are entities confined to one fold if rows are grouped?
6. Were duplicates removed before splitting?
7. Are folds stratified if classes are imbalanced?
8. How many times has the test set been used?
9. What is the majority-class or naive baseline?
10. Is the validation score too good to be true?

That last question is the one that catches real leakage most often. A score that seems too good almost always is.
